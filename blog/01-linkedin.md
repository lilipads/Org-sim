I built a simulator for organizational dynamics, played by AI agents.

Most of what we believe about organizational health comes from anecdote: a project that went well, a reorg that didn't, a meeting that should have been an email. Anecdotes are hard to argue with and impossible to rerun. In real life there's no counterfactual: you can't take the same team, change one thing, and play the quarter again. With AI agents, you can. In the simulator, agents with their own personalities and hands build a domino picture together against a clock, blind judges score what falls, and every word and move is logged. Then you change one thing about the team and rerun it, many times.

The idea came from an offsite game many of us have played: tables of people, a pile of parts, a time limit. What stuck with me was the big tables. A few people built, a few talked about building, and several hovered and offered to "help wherever."

So the first experiment is overstaffing: tables of 1, 4 and 10, with the same task and the same ten minutes.

Ten people talked 60% more than four, laid about a third fewer dominoes each, and finished later with slightly worse pictures. More than a third of them laid almost nothing. The work only had about five pieces, and every person beyond that turned into coordination, not output.

It felt very familiar. In a huge org, I commit far fewer PRs than friends at startups, and my calendar is full of meetings. Individually, the days can feel slow. The simulation suggests that isn't a people problem. It's the cost of headcount beyond the pieces of work.

Coming up: Eng, Design and PM deciding how much to talk before they build (the design review became a rubber stamp); dictators vs. consensus; how performance reviews change what people do; and what happens when everyone gets AI agents of their own, and who reads the docs those agents write.
