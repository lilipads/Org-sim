I built a simulator for organizational dynamics, played by AI agents.

Each agent gets a personality, a pair of hands of its own speed and steadiness, and a seat at a table. Tables have ten minutes to build a domino picture of an animal together: agree on a plan, sketch it, split the work, build, fix each other's breaks. Then the start domino is pushed, and blind judges guess the animal from whatever fell. Everything runs on a real-time clock where talking costs time, and every word, domino and decision is logged. That means you can change one thing about a team (its size, how it decides, how it's reviewed) and rerun it as many times as you like.

The idea came from an offsite game many of us have played: tables of people, a pile of parts, a time limit. What stuck with me was the big tables. A few people built, a few talked about building, and several hovered and offered to "help wherever."

So the first experiment is overstaffing: tables of 1, 4 and 10, with the same task and the same ten minutes.

Ten people talked 60% more than four, laid about a third fewer dominoes each, and finished later with slightly worse pictures. More than a third of them laid almost nothing. The work only had about five pieces, and every person beyond that turned into coordination, not output.

It felt very familiar. At Google, a huge org, I commit far fewer PRs than friends at startups, and my calendar is full of meetings. Individually, the days can feel slow. The simulation suggests that isn't a people problem. It's the cost of headcount beyond the pieces of work.

Next: what happens when a team of Eng, Design and PM has to decide how much to discuss before building. (Spoiler: the design review turned into a rubber stamp.)
